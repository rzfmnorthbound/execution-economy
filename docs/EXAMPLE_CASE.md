# Case Illustrations: Roles in an Execution‑Owned Enterprise

One of the most common questions from people with traditional business backgrounds is **“what added value does this model offer, and how would I experience it in my role?”**  
To bridge that gap, this document provides narrative examples for three key roles—founder, domain head and executor—to illustrate how the Execution Economy operates in practice.  
These vignettes are simplified, but they highlight the real differentiators: alignment of incentives, voice in governance, and meaningful participation in value creation.

---

## 1. A founder’s path

**Context:** You have an idea for a distributed home‑maintenance service—think electricians, plumbers and HVAC technicians connected to households through a single app. In a traditional model, you would build the software, raise capital, hire a management team and contract gig workers to deliver the services.

**In the Execution Economy:**

* You still develop the concept, launch the platform and recruit domain experts to coordinate operations. The intellectual property and brand belong to the enterprise you create.
* Instead of treating technicians as disposable contractors, you invite them to become part of the **execution network**. Each time a technician completes a job, they earn **execution units** proportional to their work and quality.  
  These units give them both economic participation in the *execution pool* and 49 % of strategic voice when key decisions require community input.
* Because voice is separated from compensation, you retain 51 % strategic authority to protect the mission and guide growth. Your economic return comes from two sources: a percentage of revenue allocated to the **founder pool**, and the long‑term appreciation of the brand and network you have built.
* When you pitch investors, you can demonstrate that your model dramatically reduces churn and marketing costs because technicians have a stake in the platform’s reputation. This alignment becomes a competitive moat.

**Value add:**  As a founder, you benefit from a more resilient workforce and higher service quality. At the same time, you avoid the PR, regulatory and retention issues faced by extractive gig platforms.

## 2. A domain head’s path

**Context:** Imagine you are an operations specialist recruited to coordinate logistics for a city‑wide delivery network. In a typical platform company, you would manage scheduling, assign drivers and optimize routes, but your authority would be limited and your compensation could be unaligned with driver performance.

**In the Execution Economy:**

* You are appointed by the founders to oversee the **operations pool** and coordinate the execution network in your domain. Your role is to translate strategy into workflows, monitor service quality and support the executors.
* You receive a competitive salary reflective of market standards and the responsibility of your position. You may also earn a performance bonus tied to network metrics (e.g. average delivery time, customer satisfaction) rather than purely revenue extraction.
* Because executors have 49 % voice in strategic decisions, you are incentivized to build trust with them. For example, you might set up feedback loops where drivers can propose efficiency improvements that you can implement directly without waiting for top‑level approval.
* You do **not** have to worry about constant renegotiation of power: strategic governance is defined by the 51/49 split, and your operational authority is recognized within that framework.

**Value add:**  The Execution Economy empowers you to run operations with a motivated network that cares about service quality. You avoid being caught between an extractive board and an unhappy workforce because both sides are structurally aligned around execution.

## 3. An executor’s path

**Context:** You are a courier joining a new delivery platform. In most gig systems, you are paid per job, bear vehicle costs, have no say in fees or policy changes, and can be deactivated without warning.

**In the Execution Economy:**

* When you sign up, you are onboarded as part of the **execution network**, not as a disposable contractor. Each delivery you complete earns you **execution units**.  
  High ratings, reliability and contributions to network improvement multiply those units. These units determine your share of the **execution pool**, which is the largest slice of revenue distribution.
* You also gain 49 % voice in strategic decisions. For instance, if the enterprise considers raising the customer service fee or expanding to a new region, your network’s vote directly influences the outcome.
* Your participation is **active**: if you stop working for an extended period without a valid reason, your economic participation gradually decays. This encourages ongoing engagement and keeps the network dynamic.
* When challenges arise (vehicle breakdowns, disputes with customers), you have a formal channel to resolve them without fear of arbitrary deactivation. The reputation system protects honest workers while discouraging gaming.

**Value add:**  You are not merely exchanging time for money; you are building a reputation and an economic stake in a platform you help sustain. The model rewards quality and long‑term commitment, giving you a reason to provide excellent service.

## Conclusion

These cases show how the Execution Economy provides tangible benefits across roles:

* **Founders** can build competitive businesses with lower churn and stronger brand loyalty.  
* **Domain heads** operate in an environment where their incentives align with both founders and executors, leading to smoother operations and fewer conflicts.  
* **Executors** gain agency, fairness and a path toward collective power without needing to become managers.

By aligning incentives around meaningful contribution and quality, the Execution Economy delivers a clear value proposition for stakeholders who might otherwise question what is gained by moving beyond traditional platform models.
